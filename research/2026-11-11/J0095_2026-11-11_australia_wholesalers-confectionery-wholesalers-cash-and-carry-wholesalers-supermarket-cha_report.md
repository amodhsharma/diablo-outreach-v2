## Market structure

Australian grocery is a tight oligopoly. The ACCC supermarkets inquiry (2025) put Woolworths at 38% of supermarket grocery sales, Coles at 29%, Aldi at 9% and Metcash supplied independents (IGA, Foodland and others) at 7%. The national buying teams of Woolworths and Coles are therefore the main gatekeepers for packaged confectionery and snacks, with Aldi working mostly on its own label. The ACCC sees Costco, Amazon and Harris Farm as the more likely challengers, and these are realistic secondary listings for an imported better for you brand. Imported brands rarely sell straight to the chains: they usually appoint a local importer and distributor (or a sales and merchandising broker) that handles ranging reviews, promotions and delivery into the chains' distribution centres (unverified for this run, common practice). Independent supermarkets, convenience, petrol and route trade stores are reached through Metcash and a small group of national and state confectionery and convenience wholesalers; most large ones in this route are already in Diablo's records. Health food stores and pharmacies form a separate, smaller channel with their own wholesalers. Online, the supermarkets' own websites and Amazon Australia carry most grocery orders (unverified).

## Barriers to entry

- Two chains hold about two thirds of supermarket grocery sales, so a missed Woolworths or Coles range review can shut a brand out of mainstream grocery for a year or more.
- The ACCC found Coles and Woolworths often accept a supplier's cost increase only when their percentage retail margin is kept or raised, paid for through a higher RRP, more promotional spend or other payments. Plan for promotional funding on top of the distributor margin.
- Since 1 April 2025 the Food and Grocery Code of Conduct is mandatory for retailers and wholesalers with over AUD 5 billion in grocery revenue: written supply agreements, good faith dealing and protection from retribution, with penalties up to AUD 10 million per breach. This helps a small supplier in disputes.
- Exclusivity norms, listing fees and distributor margin and credit terms could not be verified in this run; treat them as open questions for distributor meetings.
- Claims: Standard 1.2.7 and Schedule 4 of the Food Standards Code set the conditions for "sugar free" and "no added sugar" claims. The exact thresholds were not read in this run (unverified); check them before artwork, as they differ from the EU and UK rules. For the Code, "sugars" means monosaccharides and disaccharides, so polyols are not counted as sugars.
- Sweeteners and polyols: steviol glycosides are a permitted intense sweetener in the Code (Schedule 15). Whether every polyol and level in Diablo's range is permitted, and the exact wording and trigger level of the advisory statement for polyols (laxative effect), were not verified in this run and need checking against Schedules 9 and 15.
- Import: every consignment goes through biosecurity (BICON) and then the Imported Food Inspection Scheme; referred food is held until cleared, and inspections and tests are paid by the importer. A Food Import Compliance Agreement is an alternative for importers with an audited food safety system.
- Labelling: most imported packaged food needs a country of origin statement (for example "Product of United Kingdom") on each pack; labels can be applied before or after import. Other Australian label requirements (importer details, nutrition information panel format) apply but were not checked in this run.
- Duty: under the Australia UK Free Trade Agreement (in force since 31 May 2023) the former 5% tariff on chocolate, biscuits and chewing gum is now 0% for goods of UK origin. Diablo's goods must meet the agreement's rules of origin to qualify (not checked); goods made in the EU do not benefit, as there is no Australia EU agreement in force (unverified).
- Shelf life at entry: no verified rule found; chains and distributors usually set their own minimum remaining shelf life (unverified).
