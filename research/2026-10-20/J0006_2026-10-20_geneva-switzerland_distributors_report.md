## Market structure

Swiss grocery is a duopoly. Coop and Migros together hold roughly 70% of grocery sales, and a 2025 Nielsen based report put Coop at 43% of food and near food sales against 37.4% for Migros. Both run large own label ranges (private label is around half of sales, a figure from 2021 data) and buy many brands centrally, so they are the real gatekeepers for any national listing. Denner, Lidl and Aldi are the main discounters; Lidl plans 300 more stores. Small formats matter for snacks: Coop Pronto and Migrolino convenience stores are growing. Geneva itself is a French speaking border canton where shoppers regularly cross into France, which caps prices for everyday goods. For an imported range such as Diablo, the realistic route is a Swiss importer or distributor that holds stock, clears customs and labels for Switzerland, then sells to the chains, independent and specialist shops, pharmacies and drugstores, cash and carry and online retailers. Online took a growing share of retail (11.8% in 2020); current marketplace figures are unverified.

## Barriers to entry

- Retail concentration: Coop and Migros control most grocery shelf space and push own label hard, so a branded listing there is slow and selective. Exact listing fees and promotion contributions were not found and are unverified.
- Distributor margin, exclusivity and credit norms: no reliable public figures found; expect national exclusivity requests from importers, but treat this as unverified.
- Import duty: Switzerland abolished industrial tariffs on 1 January 2024, but agricultural and processed agricultural products such as chocolate and confectionery still carry duties with a variable agricultural component that offsets raw material price gaps.
- UK origin: under the UK Switzerland trade agreement, UK processed agricultural products face tariffs no higher than EU goods, with an EUR1 certificate or origin declaration needed. Whether every Diablo SKU meets UK origin rules (it may be made outside the UK) is unverified.
- Sweeteners: only steviol glycosides (E 960a to E 960d) are permitted as stevia sweeteners, under the FDHA additives ordinance (OAdd) revised in 2023. Polyol permissions and the laxative warning wording were not confirmed in this research; Swiss rules are said to be largely compatible with EU rules, so check each product against OAdd before shipping.
- Claims: sugar free and no added sugar claims are regulated nationally; the exact Swiss conditions were not verified here and are assumed, unverified, to mirror the EU thresholds.
- Labelling: mandatory items include country of production and the address of the manufacturer or distributor. One report says labels may be in German, French, Italian or English, but for Geneva shoppers French is strongly advisable; the English option is unverified against the ordinance.
- Importer duties: the importer must run its own checks that goods meet Swiss law; foods not listed in the food ordinance need an FSVO permit.
- Shelf life at entry: no published Swiss norm found; unverified.
