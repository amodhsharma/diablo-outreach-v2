## Market structure

Swiss grocery is held by two cooperatives: Coop has about 43% and Migros about 37% of food and related sales (Nielsen, reported November 2025). Coop is run from Basel, so its national category buying sits in the city, but listings are national and slow and both groups rely heavily on own brands. For a new brand the practical route is an importer that holds stock and sells outside them: kiosks and station shops, the Volg and Landi network, Spar, independents, health food shops, drugstores, pharmacies and online shops (which buy direct is unverified). Basel is Switzerland's main gateway for foreign trade: the canton calls the region the country's most important logistics location, and its Rhine ports alone handle about 10% of all Swiss imports, with food and farm products about 12% of their traffic (2019). Many importers and forwarders therefore sit in Basel and its suburbs (Birsfelden, Muttenz, Pratteln) and serve the whole German-speaking area.

## Barriers to entry

- Concentration: Coop and Migros together hold about 80% of food sales; listings with them are national and slow, so importers serving kiosks, convenience, health and specialist shops are the practical first step.
- Import duty: chocolate, biscuits and similar processed farm products pay an industrial element plus variable amounts that bring the cost of the farm raw materials (sugar, milk, flour) up to Swiss prices; EU goods get reduced amounts under Protocol 2 of the Swiss-EU free trade agreement. How this applies to UK goods under the UK-Switzerland agreement is unverified and should be checked per product.
- Market access: under the Cassis de Dijon principle many foods lawfully sold in the EU can be sold in Switzerland; foods that do not fully meet Swiss rules need a general authorisation from the FSVO before sale.
- Sweeteners: steviol glycosides (E 960a to E 960d) are allowed in certain foods since the 2023 revision of the Swiss additive ordinance (ZuV); each product's use should be checked against it.
- Labelling: mandatory information in at least one official language (German, French or Italian), name and address of the manufacturer, importer, packer or seller, and the country of production; national listings usually expect German and French (unverified).
- Claims: sugar-free and no-added-sugar claim conditions are understood to follow the EU thresholds, but this is unverified for Switzerland.
- Sugar policy: retailers and producers signed the voluntary Milan Declaration (2015) to cut added sugar, which favours sugar free lines; listing fees, margins and exclusivity norms are unverified.
