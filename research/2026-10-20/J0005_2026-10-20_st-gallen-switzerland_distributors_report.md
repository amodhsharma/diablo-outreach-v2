## Market structure

Switzerland is one of the most concentrated grocery markets in Europe. Coop holds about 43% and Migros about 37.4% of food and near food sales (Nielsen Retail Monitor, as reported in November 2025), so the two cooperatives' central buying teams are the main gatekeepers. Migros buys partly through regional cooperatives, which for St. Gallen is Genossenschaft Migros Ostschweiz, based in the city. Discounters Denner (Migros group), Aldi and Lidl hold most of the rest, with Lidl expanding its store count. Coop also buys through the European alliance Coopernic, which strengthens its hand in price talks. Convenience and kiosk supply is narrow: Lekkerland Switzerland was liquidated in 2020, leaving Valora and the retailers' own convenience formats as the main route. Independent shops, gastro and smaller chains in Eastern Switzerland buy through cash and carry wholesalers such as CCA Angehrn (Aligro group), Transgourmet/Prodega and TopCC, and through specialist importers who hold Swiss compliant stock. For a foreign brand the realistic route is a Swiss importer that relabels, clears customs and then sells into Coop, Migros, the discounters, drugstores and online players such as Galaxus. The split of online grocery is not verified here.

## Barriers to entry

- Retail concentration: two cooperatives control roughly 80% of food sales, so missing a Coop or Migros listing limits a brand to discounters, drugstores, independents and online.
- Buying power: Coop negotiates through Coopernic, so expect hard price talks. Listing fees, promotion contributions and distributor margin norms for Switzerland could not be verified in this run.
- Exclusivity: Swiss importers usually ask for national exclusivity because they carry the relabelling and compliance cost (common practice, not verified from a source here).
- Labelling: mandatory details must be in at least one Swiss official language (German for St. Gallen) with the name and address of the manufacturer, importer or seller; a website or QR code alone is not enough. Some reports say English is accepted, but the cantonal guidance cites only official languages, so plan for German stickers at least.
- Country of production must be stated on the pack, and Switzerland still asks for origin of main ingredients in some cases, which the EU does not.
- Claims: "zuckerfrei" and other nutrition claims are allowed only as listed in Annex 13 of the Swiss food information ordinance (LIV), and Switzerland has no procedure to approve new claims. Whether the "ohne Zuckerzusatz" wording and conditions match EU Regulation 1924/2006 exactly is unverified.
- Sweeteners and polyols: these are governed by the Swiss additives ordinance (ZuV), which the authorities update in line with EU rules. Permitted uses for steviol glycosides and polyols by product type, and the "excessive consumption may have a laxative effect" warning, are assumed to match EU law but were not confirmed in this run.
- Cassis de Dijon: products legally sold in the EU can in principle be sold in Switzerland, and foods that do not fully meet Swiss rules need an FSVO general ruling. The FSVO page refers only to EU and EEA products, so whether UK-only labelled goods qualify is unverified.
- Import duty: Switzerland abolished tariffs on industrial goods from 1 January 2024, but processed agricultural products such as chocolate and biscuits still carry duties on their agricultural component (milk powder, butter, flour). Products whose only agricultural input is sugar are duty free under the EU arrangement.
- UK trade: trade runs under the existing UK and Switzerland agreement. An enhanced deal was concluded in July 2026 but awaits ratification and its effect on confectionery is not stated.
- Shelf life at entry: no Swiss rule was found; importer norms are unverified.
