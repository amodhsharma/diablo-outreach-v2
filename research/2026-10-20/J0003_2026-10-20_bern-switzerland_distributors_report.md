## Market structure

Swiss grocery is a near duopoly. Coop holds about 43% and Migros about 37% of food and near-food sales (Nielsen figures reported in November 2025), so their central buying teams are the main gatekeepers for any brand that wants national shelf space. In Bern, Migros trades through the regional cooperative Migros Aare, which covers the cantons of Aargau, Solothurn and Bern, while Coop buys nationally. German discounters Aldi and Lidl are growing (Lidl has announced plans for 300 more stores) and keep pressure on branded prices; private label is strong across all chains. Most foreign confectionery reaches these chains and the independent, convenience, pharmacy, drugstore and foodservice trade through Swiss importers and wholesalers who clear customs, relabel where needed and hold stock, because the chains prefer a local supplier of record. Online grocery is led by the retailers' own platforms (coop.ch and Migros online, including Galaxus within the Migros group); we did not verify current online shares. For a sugar-free specialist range, a realistic first route is a specialist importer serving health food shops, drugstores, pharmacies and ethnic or British food shops in the Bern area, with a duopoly listing as a later step.

## Barriers to entry

- Duopoly buying power: Coop and Migros together control roughly four fifths of food sales, so losing or failing to win one listing closes much of the market. Promotional funding is commonly demanded from suppliers; we could not verify published listing fee levels.
- Price pressure: discounters and strong private label squeeze branded margins. Typical Swiss distributor margins and credit terms were not verified in this research.
- Import duty: chocolate, biscuits and other processed agricultural goods pay an agricultural component calculated from reference prices for ingredients such as milk, sugar and flour, which raises landed cost. EU goods get net price equalisation under Protocol 2; the treatment of UK origin goods should be checked in Tares.
- UK trade agreement: UK and Swiss trade runs under the existing continuity agreement. An enhanced UK and Switzerland deal was concluded in 2026 but was not yet in force in July 2026, and the reported gains are for meat, dairy, produce and wine, not confectionery.
- Rules of origin: preferential duty depends on UK origin rules being met; products made outside the UK may not qualify (unverified for Diablo's sourcing).
- Sweeteners: products with authorised sweeteners must state "with sweetener(s)" next to the name, and products with more than 10% added polyols must carry a laxative warning (LIV, Annex 2).
- Claims: nutrition claims such as sugar-free and no added sugar are allowed only as listed in Annex 13 of the LIV; we believe the conditions mirror the EU (sugar-free at 0.5 g per 100 g or less) but did not confirm the Annex text.
- Labelling language: a 2026 report says one national language or English is legally enough, but retailers in Bern will in practice expect German and usually French as well.
- Cassis de Dijon: food lawfully sold in the EU can be sold in Switzerland if it differs from Swiss rules only after an FSVO general ruling for that product type; whether UK made goods benefit is not stated by the FSVO.
- Shelf life at entry: no Swiss specific rule found; expect chains to require a large share of remaining shelf life on delivery (unverified).
