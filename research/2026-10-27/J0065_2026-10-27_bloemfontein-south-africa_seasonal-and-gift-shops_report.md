## Market structure

Packaged confectionery in South Africa reaches shelf mainly through the national grocery chains: Shoprite/Checkers, Pick n Pay and Spar. Spar works chiefly as a wholesaler supplying owner-operated stores, so Bloemfontein Spar owners have some say over local ranging once a brand is in the Spar system. National chains hold strong buyer power and win better trade terms than the wholesalers and buying groups serving independents. Pharmacy and health chains matter for a sugar-free range: Clicks (including a Bloemfontein CBD store) and Dis-Chem both sell sugar-free sweets and snacks. Online, Checkers Sixty60 leads on-demand grocery, followed by Pick n Pay asap!, Mr D (Takealot Group) and Spar2U. Each lists only what its parent retailer stocks, so online and in-store listings come together. Bloemfontein's gift and seasonal trade centres on malls such as Mimosa Mall. We could not verify any specialist seasonal or gift distributor in the city, and it is likely, but unverified, that most stock arrives through national distribution centres in Gauteng. The real gatekeepers are therefore a national importer holding compliant labels, chain category buyers and Free State Spar owners. Independent gift shops are probably best reached through an importer's field sales or wholesale rather than directly.

## Barriers to entry

- **Chain rebates and buyer power:** the Competition Commission's grocery inquiry found that national chains demand rebates to cover merchandising, store openings, shelf space and category management. It called these rebates entrenched. We could not verify listing fee amounts.
- **Exclusive leases:** anchor supermarkets have long held exclusivity clauses in malls. The Commission recommended phasing them out over five years and ending them at once for small and specialist stores. Current compliance is unverified.
- **Distributor margin, credit and exclusivity norms:** we could not verify typical importer margins, payment terms or exclusivity expectations for South Africa. Confirm these in partner talks.
- **Quick commerce fees:** we could not verify Sixty60 or asap! supplier fees. Listings appear to follow the parent chain's range.
- **Sweeteners:** R733 of 2012 governs sweeteners. Steviol glycosides may be labelled "Steviol Glycosides" or "Steviol Extract", and non-nutritive sweeteners must carry the words "non-nutritive sweetener" in the ingredients list. Permitted levels by product category have not been checked SKU by SKU. Inulin is not addressed in the sources we reviewed (unverified).
- **Laxative warning:** products with more than 50 g/kg of polyols must state "excessive consumption may have a laxative effect". The threshold for sugar-free chewing gum is 250 g/kg.
- **Sugar-free claim (R146):** "sugar free" is allowed only at 0.5 g or less of mono- and disaccharides per 100 g. Wording such as "x% sugar free" is barred.
- **No added sugar claim (R146):** prohibited on any product containing added sugars as defined. Check whether any range ingredients fall within that definition (unverified).
- **Draft R3337:** proposes front-of-pack warnings for high added sugar, sodium and saturated fat and for certain products with artificial sweeteners, plus limits on claims and cartoon characters for products that carry a warning. It was still a draft in July 2026. How stevia would be classed is unverified.
- **Import labelling:** labels must be in English with South African allergen declarations, metric net quantity, the local importer's name and address and country of origin. Over-stickering is accepted only in limited cases. Port Health can inspect, sample and detain consignments, and non-compliant labels are a common cause of detention.
- **Shelf life at entry:** we found no statutory minimum. The commercial norm is unverified.
- **Duty:** the SACU and Mozambique UK EPA rolled over the SADC EU EPA tariff commitments from 2021 but excludes some sensitive products. It allowed cumulation of EU inputs for three years, with possible extension, and the current status of that is unverified. Preferential rates for chocolate (1806), sugar confectionery (1704) and biscuits (1905) are unverified. Check the SARS tariff book.
