## Market structure

Swiss grocery is a duopoly. Coop holds about 43% and Migros about 37% of food and near food sales (Nielsen Retail Monitor, reported November 2025), so the two cooperatives are the main gatekeepers for any national listing in Zurich. Discounters Denner (Migros group), Aldi and Lidl follow, with Lidl planning 300 more stores. Private label is reported at about half of sales, which narrows shelf space for foreign brands in the big chains.
Impulse confectionery also moves through convenience: Coop Pronto, Migrolino (Migros, partly at Shell and Migrol stations and railway stations) and Valora's k kiosk and avec shops at stations, which matter in a commuter city like Zurich.
Foreign brands rarely sell to these chains direct. Most Swiss wholesalers act as importers, often with exclusive national rights, and handle duty clearance, storage, quality control and financing, then supply chains, kiosks, health food shops and drugstores. For Diablo, the realistic route is an importer or wholesaler first, then specialist retailers (health food, drugstores, pharmacies, online shops) that buy from them or direct, before any duopoly listing.

## Closing analysis

- Müller Drogeriemarkt (mueller.ch): the only Tier 1 retailer, with about 1,300 branded sweets lines across about 91 Swiss stores, sugar-free brands such as SilaVit and Not Guilty already on shelf and new inner-city stores opening in Zurich in 2026.
- Amavita (amavita.ch): 209 pharmacies, including Zurich main station, already sell other brands' chocolate, biscuits, wafers and bars plus stevia sweetened Ricola, a health-led shelf where Diablo's no-added-sugar range fits.
- Ketoshop.ch (ketoshop.ch): a sugar-free and low carb specialist in Thalwil whose shoppers are Diablo's exact buyer; it has sugar-free gummies and keto bars but few chocolate, biscuit or wafer lines, so the range fills a gap.

## Barriers to entry

- Duopoly power: Coop and Migros together take about 80% of food sales and lean on private label; Migros has publicly forced price concessions from big brands such as Ferrero. Listing fees and terms are not published (unverified).
- Importer exclusivity: Swiss importers commonly ask for exclusive regional or national rights; distributor margin and credit norms were not verified.
- Duty: processed agricultural products such as chocolate and cakes pay an industrial element plus a variable agricultural component that lifts raw material costs to Swiss price levels. Under the UK-Switzerland trade agreement, UK origin processed goods pay no more than EU goods; origin must be proven (EUR.1 or origin declaration; EUR-MED no longer accepted). Exact rates per tariff line not checked.
- VAT and clearance: customs agents usually pay duty and VAT at the border and bill the importer; no de minimis for these goods.
- Sweeteners: steviol glycosides (E 960a to E 960d) are permitted additives since the 2023 revision of the Swiss additives ordinance; the stevia leaf itself is a novel food needing authorisation. Product by product limits not checked.
- Claims and warnings: Swiss claim rules for "zuckerfrei" and "ohne Zuckerzusatz" and the polyol laxative warning are believed to mirror EU rules (unverified in the Swiss text this run).
- Labelling: labels must be in at least one official language (German for Zurich), with country of origin mandatory. Food that meets EU but not Swiss rules can enter under the Cassis de Dijon principle only with an FSVO authorisation; whether UK marketed food qualifies is not stated (unverified).
- Shelf life at entry: no published norm found (unverified).
