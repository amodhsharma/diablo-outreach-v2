## Market structure

Swiss grocery is highly concentrated: Migros and Coop hold around 70% of grocery retail between them, with Aldi, Lidl, Denner and Volg making up much of the rest, and private label accounts for roughly half of sales. Migros Ticino is a separate regional cooperative, so Bellinzona listings at Migros may need both the regional and the national buyer (unverified how much of the confectionery range is bought regionally). Both majors buy largely direct or through a small number of importers, so for a foreign brand the real gatekeepers are the national confectionery importers and kiosk wholesalers that already sit on their supplier lists. Outside the majors, kiosks, railway station shops and petrol station shops form a second route: Valora has historically run most Swiss kiosks (it estimated a 60% share in one older report, date unverified) and supplied about 1,100 further outlets, including Coop and petrol station shops. Ticino retail is also hit by shopping across the Italian border: Migros reported a much sharper fall in Ticino sales than in its other regions as cross-border shopping returned after the pandemic. Most distributors serving Bellinzona are national players based north of the Alps.

## Barriers to entry

- Retail concentration: with Migros and Coop at around 70% and heavy private label, shelf space for a foreign branded range is scarce; listing fees and promotional contributions are expected but no verified figures were found.
- Kiosk channel: historically dominated by Valora's network and its own wholesale supply, so independent kiosk wholesalers reach a smaller share (current share unverified).
- Cross-border shopping: Ticino shoppers buy in Italy, which caps local volumes and puts pressure on Swiss shelf prices.
- Distributor margin, exclusivity and credit norms: no verified figures found; expect importers to ask for national exclusivity (unverified).
- Regulation: Swiss food law largely follows EU rules on additives, so steviol glycosides, inulin and polyols permitted in the EU are expected to be permitted, but this was not verified against the Swiss additives ordinance in this run.
- Claims and warnings: "sugar free" and "no added sugar" conditions and the polyol laxative warning are expected to mirror EU rules (unverified in this run).
- Cassis de Dijon: products lawfully sold in the EU can in principle be sold in Switzerland, and foods from the EU that do not fully meet Swiss rules need FSVO authorisation; how UK-made goods are treated under this rule was not confirmed, so check whether Diablo's packs are also legally on sale in an EU country.
- Labelling: labels must be in at least one Swiss official language (Italian in Bellinzona); detailed Swiss labelling rules were not verified in this run.
- Import duty: most trade under the existing UK-Switzerland continuity agreement is tariff free, but food and drink exceptions remain, with compound tariffs and tariff rate quotas a barrier; processed foods such as chocolate and biscuits may carry an agricultural duty component (exact rates unverified).
- Shelf life at entry: no verified rule or common norm found.
