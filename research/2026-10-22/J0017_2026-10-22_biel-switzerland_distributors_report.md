## Market structure

Biel/Bienne, in the canton of Bern, is Switzerland's largest bilingual city (about 57% German and 43% French speakers in 2020) with an urban area of around 100,000 people, so it has no separate route to market: product reaches its shelves through national Swiss channels. Grocery is highly concentrated: Nielsen figures reported in November 2025 put Coop at 43% and Migros at 37.4% of food and food related sales, and both run central buying, logistics and large private label ranges, so they are the main gatekeepers for any branded snack. Discounters (Aldi, Lidl, Denner) and the Volg village network add further central buyers. Convenience is led by Valora (k kiosk and avec), with Migrolino and Coop Pronto at petrol stations; these buy centrally or through a small group of kiosk and confectionery wholesalers. For a foreign brand the practical first step is a national Swiss importer or distributor that already holds kiosk, independent grocer, health food or online listings. This run found no new Biel based confectionery distributor; local wholesalers were own-product makers, tobacco suppliers or firms without a website.

## Barriers to entry

- Retail concentration: Coop and Migros together hold about 80% of food sales on the November 2025 Nielsen figures, with heavy private label, so shelf space for a new branded range is scarce; listing fees and terms at the big chains are not published and are unverified.
- Bilingual labels: Swiss law requires food information in at least one official language, but Biel shoppers are split between German and French, so German and French packs are the practical norm for this city (a commercial judgement, not a legal rule).
- Exclusivity and margins: we found no published Swiss norm for distributor margins, credit terms or exclusivity; expect partners to ask for national exclusivity (unverified).
- Import duty: chocolate and other processed agricultural goods pay an industrial element plus variable components that offset raw material price gaps, so duty depends on the recipe; no flat rate could be verified.
- Trade agreement: the UK and Switzerland trade agreement is in force and UK processed agricultural goods face tariffs no higher than EU goods, but only with proof of UK preferential origin; goods only packed or labelled in the UK do not qualify.
- Claims: "sugar free" and similar nutrition claims are allowed only when they meet the conditions set by the FDHA Ordinance on Information on Foodstuffs; the exact Swiss wording for "no added sugars" is likely, but unverified, to mirror the EU conditions.
- Sweeteners and polyols: Swiss additive rules are understood to follow the EU list by product category (steviol glycosides and polyols permitted), with a laxative warning above 10% polyols; the Swiss text was not verified in this run.
- Country of production: must be stated when it is not clear from the address or the product name.
- Cassis de Dijon: food lawfully sold in the EU can enter, but a product not fully meeting Swiss rules needs an FSVO authorisation; whether UK made goods qualify is unverified.
- Shelf life at entry: no Swiss specific rule found; importers' minimum remaining shelf life requirements are unverified.
