## Market structure

Swiss grocery is a duopoly. Coop and Migros together hold most of food and near food sales (Nielsen figures for 2024: Coop 43%, Migros 37.4%), with Denner (owned by Migros), Aldi, Lidl and Volg sharing much of the rest. In Sion and the wider Valais, Migros trades through its regional cooperative, Migros Valais, and buys through the Migros group, so a regional listing still runs through central buying. Private label is unusually strong, around half of grocery sales, which narrows space for imported brands in the two big chains. Branded snacks reach shoppers outside the chains through kiosks and forecourt shops (Valora runs the k kiosk and avec networks, including about 370 avec stores planned by end 2023), independent grocers, health food shops and online shops, which are mostly supplied by national confectionery and kiosk wholesalers rather than by Sion based firms. The real gatekeepers are therefore the central buyers of Coop and Migros, Valora's buying team and a small group of national importers and wholesalers. Unverified: how far Lidl and Aldi stock imported sugar-free confectionery from third party brands.

## Barriers to entry

- Retail concentration: Coop and Migros control roughly 80% of food and near food sales and Migros leans heavily on its own brands; private label is about half of grocery sales.
- Listing fees, exclusivity norms and distributor margin and credit terms: not verified in this run; treat any figure as unknown until confirmed with a Swiss distributor.
- Import duty: chocolate, biscuits and other processed agricultural products pay variable duties that offset the gap between Swiss and foreign raw material prices (sugar, milk, flour). For EU goods this runs through Protocol 2 of the Swiss-EU free trade agreement.
- UK trade: the UK-Switzerland Trade Agreement has applied since 1 January 2021 and keeps preferential treatment for processed agricultural products. An enhanced agreement was concluded in substance on 13 July 2026 but is not yet signed or in force; its effect on confectionery is not yet published.
- Cassis de Dijon: food legally marketed in the EU can be sold in Switzerland, but food that does not fully meet Swiss rules still needs an FSVO general ruling. Whether UK made goods qualify depends on them being legally on the EU market (unverified for Diablo's range).
- Labelling: mandatory information must appear in at least one official language; in Sion that should be French in practice. The importer is responsible for compliance through self-checks, with cantonal chemists enforcing.
- Sweeteners and polyols: products with more than 10% added polyols must carry the warning "excessive consumption may have a laxative effect" (rule as reported in 2012; check the current wording in the Swiss food information ordinance). Steviol glycosides were allowed on EU conditions by 2012; current Swiss approval status per product type is unverified.
- Sugar-free and no-added-sugar claims: governed by the Swiss nutrition claim rules (LGV Art. 21 to 35 and the EDI ordinance on food information). Thresholds are believed to mirror the EU (0.5 g sugars per 100 g for "sugar-free") but this was not verified in this run.
- Shelf life at entry: no Swiss norm verified; agree minimum remaining life with each distributor.
