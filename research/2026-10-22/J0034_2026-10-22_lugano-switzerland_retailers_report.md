## Market structure

Packaged confectionery in Lugano reaches shoppers through the same national system as the rest of Switzerland, run in Ticino through regional arms. Coop and Migros dominate grocery: Nielsen data reported in November 2025 put Coop at 43% and Migros at 37.4% of food and near food sales, with Migros run locally by its own Ticino cooperative. Both lean heavily on private label (around half of sales, older figures), which narrows space for outside sweets brands. Discounters (Lidl, Aldi, Denner) are the growth challengers; Lidl plans 300 more Swiss stores. Kiosks, railway and petrol station shops are fed by specialist wholesalers and by Valora's network, so the real gatekeepers are the two cooperatives' central buyers, the discounters and the kiosk and convenience wholesalers. Health food shops, pharmacies and drugstores, sports nutrition shops and online shops are the open doors for a sugar-free specialist. Ticino has a specific weakness: shoppers cross into Italy, and Migros has cited cross-border shopping for falling Ticino sales (2023). Online took about 12% of Swiss retail in 2020 (older figure).

## Barriers to entry

- Duopoly: Coop and Migros hold about four fifths of food sales and push their own labels, so national listings are slow and hard won; listing fees and terms are not published (unverified).
- Distributor margins, credit terms and exclusivity norms for confectionery in Switzerland could not be verified in this research; expect an importer to ask for exclusivity for Switzerland or a language region (unverified).
- Cross-border shopping into Italy holds down Ticino grocery prices and volumes, so a Lugano listing must hold up against Italian shelf prices.
- Tariffs: the UK Switzerland trade agreement carries over the EU Switzerland terms, keeping preferential treatment for processed agricultural goods such as confectionery; goods need UK origin under Protocol 3, and EU inputs cannot simply pass through the UK. Exact Swiss duty per product must be checked in the Tares tariff.
- Labelling: details must be in at least one Swiss official language or English; a pack in Italian or English alone is legal, but Ticino shoppers and buyers will expect Italian. The pack needs a manufacturer or distributor address, country of production and allergens highlighted.
- Sweeteners and claims: Swiss rules are closely aligned with EU law (stevia and polyols permitted by product type; "no added sugars" means no added mono or disaccharides). Under EU rules, above 10% added polyols requires "excessive consumption may produce laxative effects"; that the Swiss threshold is identical is assumed, not verified here.
- UK made goods that differ from Swiss technical rules may need FSVO authorisation under the Cassis de Dijon route, which the regulator describes for goods sold legally in the EU; whether UK goods qualify is unverified.
- Shelf life at entry: no published minimum found; retailers set their own (unverified).
