## Market structure

Solothurn is a German speaking canton capital between Bern, Basel and Olten, so it has no separate route to market: product reaches its shelves through national Swiss channels. Grocery is highly concentrated: Nielsen figures reported in November 2025 put Coop at 43% and Migros at 37.4% of food and food related sales, and both run their own buying, logistics and large private label ranges, so they are the main gatekeepers for any branded snack. Discounters (Aldi, Lidl, Denner) and the Volg village network add further centralised buyers. Convenience is led by Valora (k kiosk and avec), alongside Migrolino and Coop Pronto at petrol stations; these buy centrally or through a small group of kiosk and confectionery wholesalers. For a foreign brand the practical first step is a Swiss importer or distributor that already holds kiosk, independent grocer, health food or online listings, with the large chains approached later through that partner. Online retail is growing, but we did not verify a current share. We found no source on Solothurn specific gatekeepers.

## Barriers to entry

- Retail concentration: Coop and Migros together hold about 80% of food sales on the November 2025 Nielsen figures, with heavy private label, so shelf space for a new branded range is scarce; listing fees and terms at the big chains are not published and are unverified.
- Exclusivity and margins: we found no published Swiss norm for distributor margins, credit terms or exclusivity; expect partners to ask for national exclusivity (unverified).
- Import duty: chocolate and other processed agricultural goods pay an industrial element plus variable components that offset raw material price gaps, so duty depends on the recipe; no flat rate could be verified.
- Trade agreement: the UK and Switzerland trade agreement is in force and UK processed agricultural goods face tariffs no higher than EU goods, but only with proof of UK preferential origin; goods only packed or labelled in the UK do not qualify.
- Claims: "sugar free" and similar nutrition claims are allowed only as listed in Annex 13 of the FDHA Ordinance on Information on Foodstuffs; the exact Swiss wording for "no added sugars" is likely, but unverified, to mirror the EU conditions.
- Sweeteners and polyols: Swiss additive rules are understood to follow the EU list by product category (steviol glycosides and polyols permitted), and the EU warning "excessive consumption may have a laxative effect" applies above 10% polyols; the Swiss text was not verified.
- Labelling: a Swiss radio report says label details must be in a national language or in English, with imports from Germany, France or Italy accepted as they are; German labels suit Solothurn, but the English only option is unverified in the ordinance itself. Cassis de Dijon lets food lawfully sold in the EU enter, but a product not fully meeting Swiss rules needs an FSVO authorisation; whether UK made goods qualify is unverified.
- Shelf life at entry: no Swiss specific rule found; importers' minimum remaining shelf life requirements are unverified.
