## Market structure

Swiss grocery is highly concentrated: Migros and Coop together hold the large majority of food retail, buying centrally and running their own logistics, so a listing with either is effectively a national decision taken at head office rather than in Zurich (exact shares unverified for 2026). Discounters Aldi Suisse, Lidl Schweiz and Denner form the second layer. The independent and convenience trade (kiosks, petrol shops, small grocers, gastronomy) is served by a handful of wholesalers and cash and carry groups such as Prodega/Transgourmet and kiosk specialists, most of which Diablo has already researched. Imported confectionery usually enters through a Swiss importer or distributor that holds stock, handles Swiss labelling and sells into retail head offices; brand owners with their own field forces cover kiosks and impulse outlets. Zurich is the largest consumption and head office hub, but the gatekeepers are national category buyers at the two cooperatives and the few wholesalers, not city level players. Online grocery is led by the retailers' own platforms (Migros Online, Coop.ch) and Galaxus, with limited room for third party marketplace sellers (unverified).

## Barriers to entry

- Retail concentration: Migros and Coop control access to most shelf space and favour their own brands; listing terms, promotional contributions and listing fees are negotiated nationally (amounts unverified).
- Saturated partner pool: the main Swiss confectionery distributors, wholesalers and kiosk suppliers are already on Diablo's contact list; this run found no new qualifying companies in Zurich.
- Distributor terms: importers expect exclusivity for Switzerland and margins that cover Swiss labelling, warehousing and listing costs (typical figures unverified).
- Import duty: Swiss tariffs on processed agricultural products such as chocolate and biscuits include an agricultural component; the UK Switzerland trade agreement gives preferential treatment for originating goods, but the agricultural component can still apply, so check each tariff line.
- Labelling: Swiss law (LIV) closely mirrors EU Regulation 1169/2011; at least one national language is required and products with more than 10% added polyols must carry the laxative effect warning.
- Claims and sweeteners: "sugar free" and "no added sugars" claims follow rules equivalent to the EU nutrition claims annex, and permitted sweeteners and polyols by product type follow the Swiss additives ordinance, largely aligned with EU lists (product by product check advised).
- Shelf life: retailers and importers usually expect substantial remaining shelf life on arrival (exact norms unverified).
