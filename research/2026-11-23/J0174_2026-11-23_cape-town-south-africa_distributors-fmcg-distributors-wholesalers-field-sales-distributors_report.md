## Market structure

South African confectionery reaches shelf through two systems. Formal retail is concentrated in a handful of national groups (Shoprite and Checkers, Pick n Pay, SPAR, Woolworths and Boxer, plus Massmart's Makro and Game) that buy centrally into their own distribution centres; SPAR runs as a voluntary trading group, so its regional distribution centres (Western Cape DC for Cape Town) are the gatekeeper rather than each store. Alongside sits a very large independent and informal trade (spaza shops, township supermarkets, forecourts and corner cafes), estimated in recent press coverage at well over R200 billion a year (figure unverified). That trade buys through cash and carry wholesalers, buying groups and field sales distributors who run van sales and merchandising, so importers usually need a national distributor with Cape Town and Gauteng depots rather than a Cape Town only partner. In Cape Town the importers of UK and European confectionery that fit Diablo are few and already known to the client; the realistic gatekeepers are the retailers' central buyers, SPAR Western Cape and the established specialist importers. Online grocery (Checkers Sixty60, Pick n Pay asap!, Takealot) is growing but is retailer controlled.

## Barriers to entry

- Listing and trade terms: national chains charge listing fees, rebates and marketing contributions; the Competition Commission's Grocery Retail Market Inquiry (2019) flagged supplier rebates and long exclusive mall leases as barriers for smaller suppliers. Exact fee levels are not public.
- Distributor terms: importers typically want exclusivity for the country and expect margin to cover warehousing, van sales and credit to retail; 30 to 60 day retailer payment terms are common (unverified for this category).
- Dominant players: Mondelez (Cadbury), Nestle, Mars, Beacon (Tiger Brands) and Ferrero hold most confectionery shelf space and run their own sales forces.
- Sweeteners: permitted sweeteners and limits are set by the sweetener regulations (R733 of 2012) under the Foodstuffs, Cosmetics and Disinfectants Act; steviol glycosides and polyols should be checked against the product type tables before shipping.
- Claims and labels: labelling follows R146 (2010); sugar-free and no-added-sugar claims have set conditions, labels must be in English and name a South African importer or distributor. A polyol laxative warning applies above a threshold (exact wording and level unverified). The draft labelling regulations R3337 (2023) propose front-of-pack warnings, including on sweeteners (final status and wording unverified).
- Duty: the SACU and Mozambique and UK Economic Partnership Agreement (in force since 2021) gives preferential access for UK origin goods; the duty on chocolate and sugar confectionery under the agreement should be checked in the tariff schedule (unverified). EU origin goods use the EU and SADC EPA instead.
- Shelf life: retailers and importers usually ask for a large share of shelf life remaining at entry (exact norm unverified).
