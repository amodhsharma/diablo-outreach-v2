## Market structure

Irish grocery is concentrated. Worldpanel data for the 12 weeks to 9 August 2026 put Dunnes Stores at 24.1%, Tesco at 23.8%, SuperValu at 19.4%, Lidl at 14.8% and Aldi at 10.9%, so five buyers control over 90% of supermarket sales. Online grocery is growing fast (sales up 27.6% in the same period) and runs mainly through those retailers' own sites.

Convenience is unusually important and is run by two wholesaler-owned symbol groups. Musgrave supplies SuperValu, Centra and Daybreak and runs MarketPlace cash and carries. BWG Foods supplies Spar, Mace and others and runs Value Centre, Ireland's largest cash and carry chain with 22 branches. Independent shops buy from these and from smaller Dublin cash and carries.

Overseas brands usually enter through an Irish distributor that holds the listings, for example Richmond Marketing for Ritter Sport. Health stores and pharmacies are served by specialists such as Wholefoods Wholesale and pharmacy wholesalers such as Uniphar.

The real gatekeepers are therefore the central buyers at the five multiples, Musgrave and BWG, plus the handful of distributors those buyers already trust.

## Barriers to entry

- Exclusivity: imported confectionery brands are commonly given to one Irish distributor on an exclusive basis (Ritter Sport and Richmond Marketing, 2024). Expect serious distributors to ask for national exclusivity; splitting grocery from health and pharmacy is possible but must be agreed up front.
- Dominant players: Musgrave and BWG are both the wholesaler and the brand owner of their symbol stores, and BWG has been buying up independent cash and carries. A central listing with either opens hundreds of stores; without one, coverage is store by store.
- Listing and promotional fees: under Ireland's unfair trading practices rules, payments for listing, stocking, promotions, advertising and marketing are allowed only if agreed clearly in advance, and payments unrelated to the sale of the products are banned. The Agri-Food Regulator enforces this. Protection depends on turnover bands, so check whether the selling entity is covered.
- Credit terms: the same rules cap payment at 60 days for non-perishable food (30 days for perishable) where they apply.
- Margins: no reliable published figure was found for Irish distributor or retailer margins on confectionery. Treat any number as unverified until quoted by a distributor.
- Sweeteners: EU additive rules (Regulation 1333/2008) allow polyols such as maltitol and erythritol in most foods only where the product is energy-reduced or has no added sugars. Check each SKU against its food category.
- Label statements: products with polyols must state "with sweetener(s)", and those with more than 10% added polyols must carry "excessive consumption may produce laxative effects" (Regulation 1169/2011).
- Claims: "sugar-free" needs no more than 0.5 g sugars per 100 g or 100 ml. "No added sugars" needs no added mono or disaccharides or other sweetening foods, and "contains naturally occurring sugars" must appear where sugars are naturally present (Regulation 1924/2006).
- Import labelling: Great Britain is outside the EU, so packs sold in Ireland need the name and address of an EU-based operator or the importer into the EU. A PO box is usually not accepted. English-language labels are assumed sufficient for Ireland; this was not separately verified.
- Duty: the EU and UK trade agreement gives zero tariffs only to goods that meet its rules of origin. Goods made in the EU or elsewhere and re-exported from Great Britain to Ireland can attract EU duty, so stock made in the EU is better shipped to Ireland direct from an EU location. Customs declarations apply to shipments from Great Britain. The duty rate and VAT treatment for each product were not verified.
- Shelf life at entry: no published Irish norm was found. Distributors generally set a minimum remaining shelf life in their terms; confirm it in the first conversation.
