## Market structure

Packaged confectionery in South Africa reaches shoppers through two very different routes. Formal grocery is concentrated in a few national chains (Shoprite Checkers, Pick n Pay with Boxer, Spar and Woolworths), which buy centrally or through regional distribution centres and sit behind supplier rebate structures. The informal channel is just as important: Accenture counts over 150,000 spaza shops, visited daily by about 80% of the population and taking about 40% of yearly food spend. Most spaza owners cannot buy directly from manufacturers and stock up from cash and carry wholesalers in limited ranges, so wholesalers are the real gatekeepers there. For an imported premium sugar free range, the realistic first shelf in Johannesburg is the better-off formal and specialty end: health stores, pharmacies, Woolworths style premium grocery, gift and seasonal retailers and online shops, usually reached through an importer distributor that clears goods and services chains. Seasonal and gift demand peaks at Christmas, Easter and Valentine's Day. Unverified: the exact share of confectionery sold through spazas versus chains, and how far gift shops in Johannesburg buy from importers rather than directly.

## Barriers to entry

- Chain power: the Competition Commission's Grocery Retail Market Inquiry found the national chains' buyer power drives a rebate based model that disadvantages wholesalers and small retailers; expect rebates and promotional contributions to get listed. Specific listing fee levels are unverified.
- Mall exclusivity: big supermarkets were told to drop exclusivity clauses in mall leases, which had kept specialty grocers out of malls; this now eases access for smaller specialty shops.
- Sweeteners (R733 of 2012): only sweeteners referenced in the Codex GSFA may be used, within GSFA maximum levels for ready-to-eat foods. Steviol glycosides must be labelled "Steviol Glycosides" or "Steviol Extract", with "non-nutritive sweetener" stated.
- Laxative warning: polyols above 50 g/kg of the final product need "excessive consumption may have a laxative effect" (250 g/kg for sugar free gum).
- Claims (R146): "free or virtually free" sugar claims need 0.5 g or less per 100 g; "no added sugar" may not be used on a product with added sugars. Only prescribed claim wording is allowed, so UK packs will need South African stickers or packs.
- Draft R3337: proposed front-of-pack warnings for added sugar, saturated fat and certain sweetened products, with limits on claims, cartoon characters and promotions on warned products. Still a draft with no implementation date as of 2026.
- Labelling: R146 requires English labelling and the local importer's details; the exact sticker rules for imported stock were not verified in this run.
- Trade terms: the SACUM-UK Economic Partnership Agreement rolled over the EU-SADC terms after Brexit, so UK origin goods may get preferential duty if they meet the rules of origin. The duty rate for sugar free chocolate and biscuits was not verified. The Health Promotion Levy applies to sugary drinks only, not to confectionery.
- Distributor margins, credit terms and shelf life demanded at entry were not verified in this run.
