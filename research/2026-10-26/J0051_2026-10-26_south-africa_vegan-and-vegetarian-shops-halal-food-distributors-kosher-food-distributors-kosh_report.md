## Market structure

Packaged confectionery in South Africa reaches shoppers through two very different routes. Formal grocery is concentrated in a handful of listed groups: Shoprite (Checkers, Shoprite, Usave), Pick n Pay (with Boxer, now separately listed), SPAR (a wholesaler supplying independently owned stores) and Woolworths Food, with Clicks and Dis-Chem adding a health and pharmacy aisle. Together these groups run more than 10,000 outlets. Central buying offices at these groups are the main gatekeepers, and an imported brand usually needs a local importer or distributor that already holds their listings and handles merchandising.
The informal route is large: more than 150,000 spaza shops, an estimated R178 billion market, visited daily by most of the population. Spaza owners mostly buy from wholesalers and cash and carry outlets in limited ranges rather than from brand owners, so wholesalers are the gatekeepers here. For Diablo's premium, sugar free range the more realistic entry points are specialist importers serving health, halal and kosher retail, health food and pharmacy chains, online health shops and corporate gifting firms, rather than mass spaza distribution (this is a judgement, not a sourced fact).

## Barriers to entry

- Retailer power: the Competition Commission's grocery inquiry found that national chains demand supplier rebates for merchandising, shelf space, store openings and category management, on rigid trading terms. Exact listing fee levels are not public (unverified).
- Dominant players: the national chains and their own labels, plus large local confectioners, hold most shelf space; independents and buying groups get weaker terms than chains.
- Distributor margin and credit norms: not verified for this range; expect importers to ask for exclusivity and marketing support (unverified).
- Sweeteners: only sweeteners in the Codex GSFA are permitted (R733 of 2012). Steviol glycosides must be labelled as "Steviol Glycosides" or "Steviol Extract", with "non-nutritive sweetener" in the ingredient list.
- Laxative warning: foods with more than 50 g/kg of polyols must state "excessive consumption may have a laxative effect".
- Claims: R146 of 2010 allows only prescribed claim wording; "free" of sugars is listed at 0,5 g per 100 g (reading this as the sugar free criterion is an inference); "no added sugar" is barred on any food with added sugars. Draft R3337 (front of pack warning labels) is expected to replace R146 but is not yet in force.
- Import labelling: mandatory information in English, a South African importer or distributor named on pack, South Africa's allergen list and metric net quantity; Port Health checks labels against R146 and can detain or destroy non-compliant stock, so overseas packs usually need over-labels.
- Permits: products of plant or animal origin generally need a DALRRD import permit before shipment, plus an ITAC permit for controlled goods.
- Shelf life at entry: no specific rule found (unverified); importers commonly expect long remaining life.
- Duty and trade agreements: the UK and SACU (including South Africa) signed the SACUM-UK EPA in 2019, carrying over the SADC-EU EPA's staged tariff cuts with sensitive products excluded. Rates for chocolate and confectionery headings were not verified.
- Sugar tax: the Health Promotion Levy (2.21c per gram above 4 g per 100 ml) applies only to beverages; no extension to confectionery was found.
