## Market structure

Packaged confectionery in South Africa reaches shelf mostly through five grocery groups: Shoprite (Checkers), Pick n Pay, Spar, Massmart (Makro) and Woolworths, which together are reported to hold about 80% of formal retail sales. Shoprite is pulling ahead of its rivals in 2026. These chains buy centrally, so their category buyers are the real gatekeepers, and an importer usually reaches them through a local importer or distributor that holds stock, handles clearance and services stores. Traditional general dealers and spaza shops still matter outside the big cities, mostly for lower income shoppers, and are served through wholesalers and buying groups rather than direct. In Pretoria, premium and imported sweets sell through mall based chains (Menlyn Park is the main hub), Woolworths, gift and hamper businesses that peak at Christmas and online gift delivery. For seasonal and gift shops the buyer is usually the owner, who buys from local importers or wholesalers rather than direct from overseas, so a South African importer is likely to be needed first. How much of this channel is independent versus chain is unverified.

## Barriers to entry

- Retailer fees: the Competition Commission's Grocery Retail Market Inquiry found national chains demand rebates from suppliers to fund display, new stores, shelf access and category management. Exact listing fee levels are unverified.
- Dominant players: the five big groups and long term exclusive mall leases (which the Commission asked malls to phase out) favour chains over independent shops, which get smaller rebates through wholesalers and buying groups.
- Distributor margin and credit norms: not verified for this run; expect the local importer to hold stock and give credit to shops.
- Labelling today: R146 of 2010 remains the law. Draft R3337 (comments closed 2023) is still not in force as of mid 2026.
- Draft R3337 risk: it would require a front of pack warning on any product containing artificial (non nutritive) sweeteners and on products above 10 g sugar per 100 g, covering about a quarter of the front of pack, plus limits on marketing to children. Whether stevia is treated as a non nutritive sweetener under the final rule is unverified, but this could hit the whole range.
- Sweeteners and polyols by product type, sugar free and no added sugar claim conditions and any laxative warning for polyols: not verified in this run; check R146 and the additives regulations with a South African regulatory consultant before shipping.
- Import labelling: labels must meet South African rules (R146), so UK or EU packs are likely to need a local sticker or relabel. Shelf life at entry: not verified.
- Import duty: the SACU and Mozambique to UK Economic Partnership Agreement has applied since 1 January 2021, so UK origin goods may get preferential tariffs; the rate for confectionery and whether Diablo's goods meet the rules of origin are unverified.
