## Market structure

Olten is a small rail and logistics town in canton Solothurn, so it has no separate route to market: product reaches Olten shelves through national Swiss channels. Grocery is highly concentrated: Migros and Coop together hold roughly 70% of the market, run their own buying and logistics and carry a private label share of around 50%, so they are the main gatekeepers for any branded snack. Discounters (Aldi, Lidl, Denner) and the Volg village network add further centralised buyers. Convenience is led by Valora (k kiosk and avec, which it described as the Swiss convenience leader by outlet count), alongside Migrolino and Coop Pronto at petrol stations; these chains also buy centrally or through a small group of kiosk and confectionery wholesalers. For a foreign brand, the practical first step is therefore a Swiss importer or distributor that already holds kiosk, independent grocer, health food or online listings, with the large chains approached later through that partner. Online accounts for a growing share of retail (about 12% of total retail sales in 2020, per Lloyds Bank Trade); we did not verify a current figure. We found no source on Olten specific gatekeepers.

## Barriers to entry

- Retail concentration: with Migros and Coop at about 70% and heavy private label, shelf space for a new branded range is scarce; listing fees and terms at the big chains are not published and are unverified.
- Exclusivity and margins: we found no published Swiss norm for distributor margins, credit terms or exclusivity; expect partners to ask for national exclusivity (unverified).
- Import duty: chocolate and other processed agricultural goods pay an industrial element plus variable components that offset the gap in raw material prices, so duty depends on the recipe; no flat rate could be verified.
- Trade agreement: the UK and Switzerland trade agreement is in force and UK processed agricultural goods face tariffs no higher than EU goods, but only with proof of UK preferential origin (EUR1 or origin declaration); goods that are only packed or labelled in the UK do not qualify.
- Claims: nutrition claims such as "sugar free" are allowed only as listed in Annex 13 of the FDHA Ordinance on Information on Foodstuffs; we did not verify the exact Annex 13 wording for "no added sugars", which is likely but unconfirmed to mirror the EU conditions.
- Sweeteners and polyols: Swiss additive rules are understood to follow the EU list (steviol glycosides and polyols permitted by product category), but we did not verify the Swiss text; products with more than 10% polyols carry the EU "excessive consumption may have a laxative effect" warning, and the Swiss equivalent is unverified.
- Labelling: Cassis de Dijon lets food lawfully sold in the EU enter Switzerland, but a product that does not fully meet Swiss rules needs an FSVO authorisation first; whether UK made goods qualify is unverified, and labels must be in at least one Swiss official language (German for Olten), which we did not confirm in a source.
- Shelf life at entry: no Swiss specific rule found; importers' minimum remaining shelf life requirements are unverified.
