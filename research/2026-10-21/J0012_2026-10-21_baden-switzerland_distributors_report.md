## Market structure

Swiss grocery is a duopoly. Coop holds about 43% and Migros about 37% of food and near food sales (Nielsen figures reported in November 2025), with Aldi, Lidl and Denner (part of Migros) as the main discounters and Lidl planning rapid expansion. Migros and Coop buy centrally and run large own label and in house manufacturing, so a foreign confectionery brand usually reaches their shelves through a Swiss importer that holds the listing, handles customs and keeps stock. Baden sits in canton Aargau between Zurich and Basel, so it is served by national importers and wholesalers based in the Zurich and Aargau logistics belt rather than by local firms. Outside the big two, the routes are cash and carry wholesale (Prodega, Transgourmet, both owned by Coop), kiosk and convenience operators such as Valora, health food and reform shops (Reformhäuser), drugstores, independent and petrol station shops, and online grocers. Specialist importers of free from and diet products are the realistic gatekeepers for a sugar free range: they choose what reaches reform shops, drugstores and the smaller chains. Unverified: the share of sugar free confectionery sold through each route.

## Barriers to entry

- Concentration: Coop and Migros together hold about four fifths of food sales, run strong own label ranges and are known for some of the highest gross margins in Europe, so listings are hard to win and margin pressure on suppliers is high.
- Coop owns the main cash and carry chains (Prodega, Transgourmet), which narrows independent wholesale routes.
- Exclusivity: Swiss importers commonly expect national exclusivity for a brand. Unverified for this category; no published norm found.
- Listing fees and margins: no reliable published figures found for Swiss listing fees, distributor margins or credit terms; treat any number as unverified until quoted by a partner.
- Import duty: industrial tariffs were abolished on 1 January 2024, but chocolate, biscuits and similar processed agricultural products are excluded and still carry an agricultural component duty based on the gap between Swiss and world commodity prices (milk, flour, butter). Products whose only agricultural ingredient is sugar are exempt under the EU arrangement.
- UK trade: the UK Switzerland trade agreement keeps preferential trade; a broader new deal was announced in July 2026 but its entry into force and any change for confectionery are unverified. UK origin rules decide whether preferential rates apply.
- Labelling: Swiss Food Information Ordinance applies; labels must be in at least one official language and in practice German, French and Italian for national listings. EU style products can enter under the Cassis de Dijon principle, but foods not meeting Swiss rules need a BLV general ruling; whether goods from the UK qualify is unverified.
- Sweeteners and claims: Swiss additive rules are closely aligned with the EU; steviol glycosides and polyols are permitted in many categories but food by food permissions sit in the Swiss additives ordinance annex and were not verified for each product type. "Sugar free" and "no added sugar" claims follow EU style conditions. The laxative warning is confirmed for polyol sweetener preparations; the rule for finished foods above 10% polyols is believed to mirror the EU but is unverified.
- Shelf life: buyers usually want a good share of shelf life left at delivery; no Swiss specific figure verified.
