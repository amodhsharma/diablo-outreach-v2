## Market structure

Swiss grocery is a duopoly: Coop holds about 43% and Migros about 37% of food and near food sales (Nielsen Retail Monitor, reported November 2025), with Denner (Migros group), Aldi and Lidl taking most of the rest. In Ticino the Migros regional cooperative (Migros Ticino) and Coop dominate the Lugano area, alongside Manor food halls, Denner, Aldi, Lidl and Volg or Spar convenience formats. Both majors buy centrally, so a national listing decision, not a Lugano one, is the real gatekeeper; regional Migros cooperatives have some local range freedom (unverified for snacks). Foreign confectionery usually reaches shelf through a Swiss importer that handles customs, labelling compliance and the listing negotiation, then supplies the chains, kiosks and convenience (Valora), pharmacies and drugstores, health food stores and online shops. Ticino retail is unusually exposed to shopping tourism into nearby Italy: Migros reported sharp Ticino sales falls once cross-border shopping resumed after the pandemic. This makes Lugano price sensitive and favours Italian speaking importers who can sell across the canton and into German speaking Switzerland.

## Barriers to entry

- Channel lock up: Coop and Migros together hold about 80% of food sales and have a history of hard negotiation with international brands, including Coop delisting 95 branded lines over pricing in 2011. Listing fee levels were not verified.
- Price pressure: Ticino shoppers buy across the border in Italy; the duty and VAT free allowance for private imports was halved to CHF 150 per person per day from January 2025, which helps local retail slightly.
- Distributor norms: exclusive national distribution is common for foreign brands in a small market; margin and credit terms were not verified in this run.
- Import duty: chocolate, biscuits and confectionery (HS chapters 17, 18 and 19) were NOT covered by the abolition of Swiss industrial tariffs on 1 January 2024 and remain dutiable, often with weight based or compound duties.
- UK trade deal: the Switzerland UK Trade Agreement (in force since 1 January 2021) continues preferential treatment for processed agricultural products; an enhanced FTA was concluded in substance on 13 July 2026 and is expected to be signed by end 2026. UK origin rules must be met to claim preference.
- Cassis de Dijon: products lawfully sold in the EU can in principle be sold in Switzerland; foods not meeting Swiss rules need an FSVO general ruling. Whether UK made goods qualify was not confirmed, so treat as unverified.
- Labelling: country of production must be stated; Swiss rules are largely aligned with the EU. Mandatory text in at least one Swiss official language is the usual requirement (unverified in this run); Italian labelling suits Lugano.
- Claims and sweeteners: the Swiss Food Information Ordinance (LIV, Annex 13) lists "sugar free" and "no added sugar" claims; conditions are understood to mirror EU rules (0.5 g sugars per 100 g for sugar free) but were not verified. A polyol laxative warning and "with sweeteners" statement are expected as in the EU, also unverified.
- Shelf life at entry: no verified requirement found; importers typically expect long remaining life given duty and listing lead times (unverified).
